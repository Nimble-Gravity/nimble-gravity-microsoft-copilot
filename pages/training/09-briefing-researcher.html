Briefing Researcher

With an agent that works alone for 45 minutes, the brief is where your skill lives. A vague mission returns a vague report — and costs the same query. This lesson is the briefing craft: structure the mission like a memo to a sharp new hire, engage seriously with the clarifying questions, and handle the report like a reviewer, not a reader.

The Researcher Agent · ~15 min read
01 — The brief is the skill

Write it like a memo to a sharp new hire

A good Researcher brief reads like the note you'd leave a talented analyst on their first day: capable of anything, but knowing nothing about your context. Four things belong in every mission — and they'll look familiar from the Module 1 prompt framework.

01
Objective
The question the report must answer, stated as a question or a decision. "Produce a diligence brief on revenue quality and earn-out risk" — not "research this company."
02
Scope & sources
Work data, web, or both — and which sources to prefer. Name the files by folder, and name the kind of web sources that count: regulator filings, carrier results, market reports.
03
Deliverable spec
Headings, length, and the citation requirement, stated explicitly. "Structured report with headings and citations, under six pages" turns a wandering essay into a workpaper.
04
Audience
Who reads it and what they'll do with it. "For the finance deal team ahead of a go/no-go" sets depth, tone, and what gets flagged versus footnoted.
The weak version — one query spent, little back 
Research the Lighthouse acquisition and tell me if it's a good deal.
The full Lighthouse brief — the one you ran in Module 1 
I'm on the finance team at a large US insurance brokerage, evaluating an acquisition target. Using the Project Lighthouse files in my OneDrive Copilot Workshop folder (the CIM extract, 3-year financials, carrier contract summary, and earn-out term sheet) AND public web sources on the Florida coastal property insurance market, produce a diligence brief that covers: (1) the quality of the target's revenue growth, separating contingent commissions from core commissions; (2) carrier concentration risk and which single relationship most threatens contingent income; (3) how current Florida coastal property market conditions could affect the proposed earn-out; (4) the five questions finance should ask before close. Structured report with headings and citations; keep it under six pages.

Spot the four elements: objective — a diligence brief answering four numbered questions; scope & sources — named work files plus a defined slice of the web; deliverable — headings, citations, under six pages; audience — finance, evaluating a target. The weak version has none of them, and it costs exactly the same query from your monthly 25.

🔎
Before you read the report — check who it cited

This is the brief you ran in Module 1. When that report is in front of you, don't start with the conclusions — scan the citation list first. Does it name the four work files and a real slice of the Florida property market, or has it leaned on one source for everything? A thin or off-scope citation list is your cue to tighten the brief and rerun, not to read on trusting it.

02 — The clarifying phase

The interview before the run

Before Researcher starts, it asks clarifying questions. Engaging with them is Microsoft's own #1 stated best practice for the agent — and it's the highest-leverage minute of the whole mission. Skip it, and the agent fills the gaps with guesses you'll pay for in the report.

Why it asks

Intake, exactly like an analyst's

The questions are the agent negotiating scope before committing 10–45 minutes of work. Every answer you give narrows the search space and sharpens the report. Treat "looks good, just run it" as leaving quality on the table.

A sharp new hire who asks scoping questions isn't slow — they're good.
Good answers

Scope, geography, depth, exclusions

The strongest answers settle four things: scope (work files and web, or one of them), geography ("US market focus — Florida coastal property specifically"), depth ("decision-ready brief, not a literature review"), and exclusions ("skip life & health carriers; ignore pre-2023 market commentary").

In the Module 1 kickoff you answered: work files and web, US focus, finance audience.
03 — Working with the output

Report in hand — now work it like a reviewer

The report is a first draft from a fast, well-read analyst — not a finished workpaper. Three habits turn it into something your name can go on, and the third one is a compliance move, not just a convenience.

01
Verify the citations
Citations are claims about sources. Open a sample and confirm the source actually says what the report says it does — the same discipline you'd apply to a junior teammate's footnotes. Lesson 4 makes this a structured pass.
02
Follow up in the same session
The session keeps the mission's context, so ask follow-ups there: "expand section 2," "what contradicts this?", "tighten the earn-out analysis to one page." Refining in-session beats commissioning a fresh run.
03
Save it into governed files
Agent session content is not e-discoverable by default — unlike regular Copilot chat history, which Purview can search. If the report matters, get it into a Word file in OneDrive or SharePoint, where retention and eDiscovery apply. The report isn't a record until you make it one.
🚦
When not to use Researcher — budget discipline

A quick fact ("what's our fiduciary cash balance per the Q1 release?") is a Copilot Chat question. Math inside a single workbook belongs to Excel Copilot — or Analyst, once you meet it in Module 4. Researcher is for multi-source synthesis questions that deserve minutes of work. Every run draws from the shared 25-query monthly pool, so spend it where synthesis is the job.
